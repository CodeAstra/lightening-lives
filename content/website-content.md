**On "single drop".** The poster outranks the brief, and the company asked for "a single drop of blood". This file still does not use it. The brief's reason holds: a company that counts drops in its headline invites comparison with Theranos, whose slogan was "One tiny drop changes everything". The file says "a spot of blood on a card" where it is speaking plainly, and "dried blood spot" where it is being exact. Both stay true whatever a test's instructions say about the number of drops. The company approved Design 10 with the earlier headline, so it has to agree to this change (Part 7, item 1).

**On the poster's large claims.** "Limitless", "any genetic test is possible", "revolutionizing" and "endless possibilities" are not in the copy either. They are statements a clinician or a regulator can ask the company to prove. What the pages say instead is the version the company can stand behind: it develops a test for any inherited condition a programme needs.

### 1.5 What every page should leave behind

In this order. A page that has room for only one says the first.

1. Genetic testing from a spot of blood on a card.
2. No DNA extraction step, a modest laboratory, a cost that works at population scale.
3. The proof: validated by ICMR, built with CSIR-CCMB know-how, developed for a national mission.
4. Any inherited condition. Five tests so far, and the next can begin with the reader's institution.
5. The company works with institutions, and most work begins as a collaboration.

### 1.6 Voice

Clear, evidence-led and warm. Short sentences and plain verbs. The reader is a professional who has heard every claim before.

- **Say the thing itself.** "A lancet, a card and a drop of blood", not "an innovative sampling solution".
- **Put proof beside the claim.** A sentence that says a test works is followed by who validated it.
- **Say where, not "underserved".** "Districts a long way from a genetics laboratory" tells the reader more than a label does.
- **The sample is "a spot of blood on a card", or a "dried blood spot".** Never "a single drop" or "one drop of blood". "One card" is fine: the card is always one.
- **Collection is "minimally invasive".** Never "painless".
- **Indian English.** Anaemia, thalassaemia, haemophilia, programme, organisation, counselling, personalised. Proper names keep their own spelling (Thalassemia and Sickle Cell Society).
- **No prices, procurement figures or clinical performance figures.**
- **No patient names, and no photographs in which a patient or community member can be identified.** Staff, founders and advisers appear by name with their own portraits, and with their consent.
- **Headings** are sentence case with no full stop. The home page's main heading and the film's three captions keep theirs.
- **Buttons** start with a verb and say what happens.

Words to leave out: revolutionary, limitless, endless, cutting-edge, world-class, empower, seamless, hope, humbled, "diagnostic tool", flagship.

#### From card to result (*steps*, numbered because the order is real)

- **H2:** From card to result

1. **Collect.** A health worker cleans the skin with an alcohol swab, pricks it with a single-use lancet and lets the blood fall into the dashed circle on the card.
2. **Dry and send.** The spot dries, and the cover closes over it. The card's barcode ties the sample to the person's record, and the back has room for their name, age, sex and the date. Closed cards go to the laboratory together. `[TBC: storage and transport conditions]`

- **A 3D view.** Each box is shown as an object: seen from above and to one side, so that the top cover, the front and the left side are all in view.

- **Where.** On each test's page under "In the box", and once as a row on the Solutions page under "How a test arrives". Never on the home page.
- **Size.** Small beside the text it illustrates. The page is about the test; the box is how it arrives.
- **Described for screen readers.** For example: "The LitLife™-SCA box: a pale pink carton with a red DNA helix, the Lightening Lives and CSIR-CCMB logos, and the words 'Dried blood spot based molecular kit for diagnosis of sickle cell anaemia'."

The current site has seven pages and six staff profiles. Read against the brief, three things stand out. It speaks as a charity: its opening slides, its "Impact stories" and its services to individuals all say so. It never mentions the company's strongest proof: LitLife™, the ICMR validation, REASSURED and the dried blood spot card do not appear anywhere on it, and CSIR-CCMB appears only as a logo and in two profiles. And what it gives search engines to show under each result is either nothing or the purchased template's own advertisement ("Laboix is beautifully designed laboratory & research HTML template…"). What it does have, and the brief does not, is the people: the founders, the team, the patron, the advisers and the collaborators. Those are carried over. The rest is below.

| On the current site | What happens to it | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The three opening slides: "Bringing Hope Through Science", "Every Life Matters", "Science Made Accessible" | Gone. "Every Life Matters" stays where it belongs, with the logo | They are the charity voice the company wants to leave behind |
